# 51. Salarias fasciatus (lawnmower / jeweled / algae blenny): color change and shelter

Scope: background matching, mood coloration, and shelter use, for the ReefLive sim. Tags:
[S] sourced (reference, paper, or database), [A] anecdotal hobbyist/retailer, [E] estimate by the author of this note.

Bottom line: the species-specific evidence is thin. FishBase (the most neutral source) says nothing about color change
or shelter. Background matching and "dark/pale with mood" are repeated across hobby sites, but no page gives
direction, magnitude, or timing for Salarias. Timing ranges below rest on general teleost physiology.

## 1. Background matching

- [S] Wikipedia: the species "camoflauges itself with its surroundings, even changing color to hide from predators."
- [S] FishBase: body "typically olive to brown with dark bars" and many white spots. Pattern: "Numerous pale spots,
  dark streaks anteriorly, and several dusky bands." Wild habitat is mixed coral, sand, and rubble on reef flats,
  shallow lagoons, and seaward reefs, with algae-rich rubble.
- [A] Aquarium guides (AquariumSource): the fish "help them blend into the reef bottoms they comb for algae," and
  tones shift "to better hide it among the rock and substrate." One source says the fish can lighten the front or
  back half of its body depending on background.
- [A] Dark vs. pale direction on dark rock or pale sand is not stated for Salarias in any source found. The general
  rule is [S] for teleosts: on a light background the fish is paler (chromatosomes aggregate); on a dark background
  it darkens (chromatosomes disperse) (Australian Museum, "Can fishes change colour?").
- [A] Resting pattern is described as pale-to-white with 7 dark vertical bars (one source) or tan/green/gray with
  darker stripes (another). The mottled brown/green/white pattern is also reported.
- [E] Working assumption: dark rock pushes the tone toward dark, pale sand pushes it toward pale, and the bars stay
  visible in both. Keep the bars as a fixed pattern layer with a modulated contrast.

Speed:
- [S] Australian Museum: rapid color change is "largely under control of the nervous system"; slow changes (such as
  breeding color) are "generally under the control of hormones and are usually semi-permanent."
- [S] 2020 review (Pigment Cell & Melanoma Research 34:564-574, seen via abstract only): teleost background
  adaptation has a fast "physiological" response (moving pigment organelles within existing chromatophores) and a
  slow "morphological" response (changing pigment cell number or type).
- [S] Fujii (1993, Int Rev Cytol 143:191-255, cited but not read): aggregation is sympathetic-nervous and
  endocrine controlled, and depends on microtubule motors.
- [S] A 2024 coral trout study (abstract seen in search): capture-induced blotches appeared within 4-5 s.
- [E] Pigment aggregation/dispersion in a fish's skin is generally on the order of seconds to minutes. Full
  background adaptation of a whole body is generally tens of seconds to a few minutes.
- [A] Aquarists report slow shifts over weeks after introduction as the fish "settle[s] into their new home."

## 2. Mood, stress, and other states

- [A] AquariumSource: the fish "experience color change based on mood"; "changes in its shading can signal stress
  or health problems."
- [A] FurryCritter (editorial, the site says its health content is opinion): color "may intensify or change somewhat
  with mood." It lists hiding and clamped fins as the stress signs, not a color pattern. Constant hiding is
  "signaling stress or illness."
- [S] No source found gives the direction (pale or dark) of stress or fear coloration for Salarias. Treat the
  direction as unknown.
- [A] Fear/startle: the fish dashes into a hole. No color change during the dash is reported.
- [A] Aggression/territorial: no Salarias color pattern is documented. The related black-faced blenny (a different family and genus, not Salarias) turns yellow with a black head in
  territorial males [A]. Other
  Labrisomid blennies suppress conspicuous colors when threatened [S, different genus]. Do not transfer these.
- [A] Breeding males: "develop enhanced coloration during breeding condition" (FurryCritter). A breeder registry
  describes olive bars with reddish-orange mottling on the dorsal fin, more pronounced in males during courtship,
  and several orange spots and speckles on the dorsal fin in spawning condition.
- [S, related species] Salaria pavo (peacock blenny): males spend more time in nests and monopolize them when nests
  are aggregated (J Fish Biol, 2009). General blenny courtship includes head bobbing at the cave mouth and leading
  the female to the nest.
- [A] Night: "Their coloration changes, especially from day to night" (search snippet, source not confirmed). No
  source gives a night color for Salarias. Fish sleep in rock crevices at night [A].
- [A] New arrival: pale or washed-out color while adjusting; the tones deepen as the fish settles.
- [A] Sick or stressed: shading changes that persist are a warning sign. No direction is given.

## 3. Shelter and hiding

- [S] FishBase: adults live on reef flats, shallow lagoons, and seaward reefs; "usually solitary, found in weedy
  areas on rocks and coral outcrops"; sites are a mix of coral, sand, and rubble. FishBase does not mention caves,
  holes, or crevices.
- [A] FurryCritter: "the caves and crevices Lawnmower Blennies use for shelter"; setups need "caves, crevices, and
  overhangs where blennies can shelter and perch"; males establish nesting territories "in holes or crevices."
- [A] FurryCritter: the fish will "dash between rocks when startled" and "dash into crevices when threatened."
- [A] AquariumSource: "They will hide in the rocks when they feel threatened and at night when they sleep." Owners
  see them "faces sticking out from a crevice or gap." They withdraw when larger or aggressive fish come near.
- [A] Hobbyist guide: when startled, the first instinct is to "madly dash for the nearest hole or cave." Hobbyists
  report the fish digging and rearranging rock to make a pit for itself.
- [A] Reef-tank guide: a tank with live rock full of "holes, ledges, and crevices" is essential.
- [A] Territorial over a prized cave, and it will hide from a larger fish (e.g., a puffer) during the first week.
- [E] Hole size: the fish is about 12 cm (one source gives 5 in). It needs gaps wide enough to enter and turn,
  so bore holes of a few mm will not hold it. Use crevices and overhangs at body-width scale.
- [E] Field analog: reef flat rubble and algae-covered rock, where the fish feeds on rock surfaces and retreats
  into gaps. Use gaps and rubble, not open sand.

Aquarium tank notes (for sim realism):
- [A] Keep one per tank (aggressive to other blennies and algae competitors); a pair fights to the death.
- [A] A new tank needs algae growth first, or the fish starves and takes to dried food or nori.

Shelter model:
